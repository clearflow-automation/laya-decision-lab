02 — WHAT WE LEARNED

Speed was the easy part.
Quality needed evidence.

A quick choice is useful only if the model sees the right state, the actions mean what we think they mean, and the choice improves an outcome we care about.

01 / INPUTS

The options carry a lot of the intelligence.

The Snake controller describes each direction as blocked, unsafe, safe, or a route toward food. Laya chooses among those labelled options. This demonstrates decision routing with structured context; it does not show Laya discovering a winning Snake strategy from raw pixels.

02 / GAME RESULT

A real run, with a visible limit.

In the featured 120-move run, the snake ate three fruit and was still alive at the cap. The replay shows every move and score. Survival and fast decisions alone do not establish strong play across games.

03 / WEIGHT ADAPTATION

Changing weights did not improve validation choices.

Separate from the game, we adapted Laya’s native scorer in a small, local trading research pilot. Its training loss fell, yet all 124 validation actions were unchanged. That pilot does not establish added decision value from the adaptation.

04 / BETTER CONTEXT

More input was not automatically better.

Another small offline pilot added historical setup statistics to the decision context. On its sampled validation dates, selection became worse than the matched version without that evidence. Relevant, causal inputs need to earn their place in a frozen comparison.

03 — WHAT COMES NEXT

Keep the experiment honest.

Next: compare Laya with Jev on the same clearly defined decision tasks once Jev inference access is available. Then continue testing whether a decision layer helps with trading research, using chronological outcomes and baseline comparisons. Early trading pilots have not shown reliable improvement.

This is a research prototype, not a trading system or investment recommendation.
